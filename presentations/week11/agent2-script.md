### Slide 6 — Revision is Not Editing
**Section:** Revision Principles
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~2 min

**Frame title:** Revision is Not Editing

**On-slide content:**
Most students conflate these. They are not the same thing.

| | Revision | Editing |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Argument, structure, integration | Sentence, paragraph, citation |
| Question | *Does the thesis hold together?* | *Is this prose clear?* |
| Output | Sections moved, rewritten, cut | Cleaner sentences |
| When | First | Last |

(Alertblock, "The trap")
It is satisfying to polish prose. It feels like progress. But polishing a paragraph you may delete is wasted effort. Always do the structural work first.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
Editing is sentence-level polish. Revision is re-seeing. They look similar from the outside but they are completely different kinds of work, and they need to happen in the right order. If you start by editing prose, you will be reluctant to cut paragraphs you have already polished — and you will protect material that does not earn its place. Do the hard structural work first.

**Transition to next slide:**
The first practical tool: global vs. local.

### Slide 7 — Global Before Local
**Section:** Revision Principles
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~2 min

**Frame title:** Global Before Local

**On-slide content:**
**Global revision** asks:
- Does the empirical chapter answer the question posed in the introduction?
- Does the literature review actually motivate the framework?
- Are the chapters in the right order?
- Is anything missing? Anything redundant?

**Local revision** asks:
- Is this transition clear?
- Does this citation point to the right page?
- Is this sentence doing the work it should be doing?

(Block, "Order matters")
Global problems cannot be fixed by local edits. A weak link from evidence to claim is not a transition problem; it is an argument problem. Diagnose at the right level.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
A useful sequence: read the whole draft once, top to bottom, without making any changes. Then write down — in plain language — what is and isn't working at the level of argument. Only after that do you let yourself touch sentences. Most marks lost on BA theses are lost at the global level: integration failures, conclusions that just restate findings, empirical chapters that drift from the question. These are not things you can fix by tightening prose.

**Transition to next slide:**

### Slide 8 — Reverse Outlining
**Section:** Revision Principles
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~3 min

**Frame title:** Reverse Outlining

**On-slide content:**
A reverse outline shows you what your draft *actually does* — not what you intended.

**The method:**
1. Take your draft.
2. For each paragraph, write a single sentence describing what that paragraph *does* (e.g., "introduces the second case," "extends a counter-argument," "summarizes the source").
3. Read those sentences as a list, in order.
4. Look at the list. The structural problems will jump out.

**What to look for in the list:**
- Two paragraphs doing the same thing → redundancy
- A claim that has no paragraph supporting it → gap
- Out-of-order moves → restructure
- Paragraphs you cannot describe in one sentence → suspect; cut or rewrite

(Exampleblock, "Try this with one section this week")
Take your introduction, or your empirical chapter, and reverse-outline it. Twenty minutes. Then look at the list and ask: is this the argument I think I am making?

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
This is the single most useful revision tool I know. It's also the one students consistently skip because it feels like extra work — but it is *less* work than rewriting later, because it surfaces the structural problems before you have invested time in polishing. Try it on one section between today and when my feedback arrives. You will find at least two things you didn't expect.

**Transition to next slide:**

### Slide 9 — Does Each Paragraph Earn Its Place?
**Section:** Revision Principles
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~1.5 min

**Frame title:** Does Each Paragraph Earn Its Place?

**On-slide content:**
For every paragraph in your draft, you should be able to answer, in one sentence:

> *What is this paragraph doing for the argument?*

If you cannot answer:
- The paragraph may be doing real work that you have not yet articulated — write the sentence, then rewrite the paragraph to do it.
- Or the paragraph is filler. Cut it.

(Block, "A useful corollary")
A paragraph is a unit of argument, not a unit of length. If two paragraphs are doing the same job, merge them. If one paragraph is doing two jobs, split it.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
This is a cheap, fast test. It works at the paragraph level the same way reverse outlining works at the section level. Every paragraph either does something for the argument or it doesn't, and the test forces you to be honest.

### Slide 13 — The Three Reading Prompts
**Section:** Peer Review
**Type:** activity instructions (handout-style)
**Time budget:** ~1.5 min (then 8 min silent reading)

**Frame title:** Three Reading Prompts

**On-slide content:**
While you read your partner's section, annotate against these three prompts. Write directly on the page or in a separate note.

**1. Argument trace.**
In one sentence: what is the central claim of this section? Can you locate where the evidence supports it? Mark the sentence(s) where the claim appears, and the sentence(s) where the evidence appears.

**2. Integration check.**
Does this section visibly connect to the research question and the analytical framework laid out earlier in the thesis? Where does the connection feel weak, missing, or assumed?

**3. One thing to keep, one thing to change.**
- Name a specific paragraph or move that works. Why?

Structural feedback is more useful than prose feedback at this stage. Save line-editing for later.

### Slide 18 — The Decision-Letter Mental Model
**Section:** Dealing with Feedback
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~2.5 min

**Frame title:** Treat Feedback Like a Decision Letter

**On-slide content:**
Academic publishing has a useful discipline. When a journal sends back a reviewer report, the experienced author follows three steps:

1. **Read it once.** Do not respond. Do not start fixing. Do not email anyone.
2. **Set it aside for 24 hours.** Sleep on it. The first read is emotional; the second is analytical.
3. **On the second read, take notes — not actions.** What is the reviewer actually asking for? Sort comments into a list before you touch the manuscript.

(Alertblock, "The 24-hour rule")
Do not start revising on the day you receive feedback. You will overweight the negative comments, miss the positive signal, and waste effort on the wrong things.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
This is a discipline I learned from publishing, and I recommend it to you wholesale. The first read is always emotional, even when the feedback is mostly positive. Twenty-four hours costs you nothing. You still have three weeks. Use the first day to absorb, not to act.

### Slide 19 — Separate the Work from the Self
**Section:** Dealing with Feedback
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~1.5 min

**Frame title:** Separate the Work from the Self

**On-slide content:**
The feedback is on the manuscript. It is not on you.

This is easy to say and hard to do. Three reframes that help:

- **Confusion is data.** When a reader is confused, that tells you something about the writing — even when the reader is wrong about the substance. Confusion is almost always a signal.
- **Disagreement is allowed.** You do not have to accept every comment. But you do have to make a defensible choice for each one.
- **The reader is not your enemy.** I am reading carefully because the manuscript is worth reading carefully. Critical feedback is a sign of attention.

(Block, "A useful question")
"What would I do with this comment if it had come from a stranger I respected?"

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
You will read comments that sting. That is normal. The reframe that helps me, and that I recommend to you, is the question on the slide: what would I do if this came from a stranger I respected? The answer is almost always: take it seriously, think about it, decide. That is the right posture.

### Slide 20 — High-Leverage Triage
**Section:** Dealing with Feedback
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~2.5 min

**Frame title:** Not All Feedback Is Equal

**On-slide content:**
Sort every substantive comment into one of three buckets. Address them in order.

| Tier | Type | Examples | When to address |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Structure / argument | "The empirical chapter does not answer the question," "the framework is not used in the analysis" | First. Highest leverage. |
| 2 | Evidence / citations | "This claim is unsupported," "this source is missing," "this quote needs context" | After Tier 1. Often quick wins. |
| 3 | Prose / style | "Awkward sentence," "comma splice," "unclear referent" | Last. Only after the rest is settled. |

(Alertblock, "The common failure mode")
Students invert this order, because Tier 3 is easier to act on. Resist this. A comma fixed in a paragraph that ends up cut is wasted time.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
The triage matters because your time is finite. You have three weeks. If you spend the first week on prose, you will not have time to fix structural problems — and structural problems are where the marks are. Do the hard work first. The prose pass is fast once the structure is right.

**Transition to next slide:**
What to do with comments you disagree with.

### Slide 21 — When You Disagree
**Section:** Dealing with Feedback
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~2 min

**Frame title:** When You Disagree with a Comment

**On-slide content:**
You are allowed to disagree. You are not required to accept every suggestion.

But: **a disagreement requires a defensible choice.**

A useful rule:

> *If a reader misread something, that is almost always a writing problem, not a reading problem.*

Two responses to consider:

- **Rewrite for clarity.** Often the reader read carefully and still misunderstood. The text is doing something it should not be doing. Fix it.
- **Add a signpost.** A single sentence — "What follows builds on the framework introduced in Chapter III…" — can prevent a misreading without major surgery.

(Block, "What not to do")
Do not write a rebuttal in the margin. Do not assume the reader was sloppy. The reader is a stand-in for every future reader of your thesis — including the second reader.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
This is the part students find most disorienting. You disagree with a comment, you think the reader missed the point — what now? My honest view: when a careful reader misreads something, the writing was almost always partly responsible. A signpost or a rewrite is faster and more useful than defending what you wrote. Save the defending for cases where you genuinely think the comment is wrong on substance — and even then, make a defensible choice and move on.

**Transition to next slide:**
The working method — addressing comments systematically.

### Slide 22 — A Working Method
**Section:** Dealing with Feedback
**Type:** content
**Time budget:** ~1.5 min

**Frame title:** A Working Method for the Feedback

**On-slide content:**
For each substantive comment, write three things in a separate document (a simple table works):

| What the reader said | What I will do | Where in the manuscript |
|---|---|---|
| (paste or paraphrase the comment) | (the action: rewrite, cut, add, signpost, leave) | (page / section / paragraph) |

This forces you to:
- **Act, not ruminate.** "Worry about this comment" is not an action.
- **Track progress.** You can see what is done and what remains.
- **Justify every disagreement.** "Leave" is a valid action, but you write down the reason.

(Exampleblock, "Why this works")
This is what professional revision looks like. It is also what you will do for any future graduate program, journal submission, or job-related writing. It is a transferable habit.

**Speaker notes / what Steven says:**
Three columns. Comment, action, location. That's it. A simple Word table or a spreadsheet. The act of writing the action down — not just thinking it — is what converts feedback into revisions. Without this, comments stay as a vague cloud of anxiety. With this, they become a checklist.
