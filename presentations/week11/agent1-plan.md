## c) Content Guidance for Agent 2

### Section 2 — Revision principles (12 min)

Cover, with substance:
- **Revision ≠ editing**: Revision is re-seeing the argument and structure. Editing is sentence-level polish. Most students conflate the two and skip the harder work.
- **Global vs. local revision**: Global = does the argument hold? Are the chapters in the right order? Does the literature review actually motivate the empirical chapter? Local = clarity, transitions, citations, prose. Always do global first. Polishing prose in a paragraph you may delete is wasted effort.
- **Reverse outlining**: After a draft, write one sentence summarizing what each paragraph *actually does* (not what you intended). Then read those sentences as a list. Gaps, redundancies, and structural failures jump out.
- **The "Does it earn its place?" test**: For each paragraph, ask: what is this doing for the argument? If you cannot answer in one sentence, it is suspect.
- **Map revision against the Protocol's four criteria**: When revising, ask each criterion in turn — does the writing show knowledge and insight? Application? Reaching conclusions? Communication? This connects revision back to how the thesis will be assessed.
- **Critique-the-argument vs. critique-the-prose**: Distinguish these explicitly. Argument-level problems (a missing premise, a weak link from evidence to claim, an underjustified scope condition) cannot be fixed by sentence rewrites. Prose-level problems (clunky transitions, unclear referents) can.
- **Anchor in BA-thesis reality**: Most BA theses lose marks on integration (does the empirical chapter answer the question posed in the introduction?) and on conclusions (does the discussion go beyond restating findings?). These are revision problems, not writing problems.

### Section 4 — Dealing with feedback (12 min)

Cover:
- **The decision-letter mental model**: Borrowed from peer-reviewed publishing. Treat written feedback as a "decision letter": read it once, set it aside for 24 hours, then read it again with a list. Do not respond emotionally on first read.
- **Separating ego from work**: Feedback is on the manuscript, not on you. The reader's confusion is data — even when the reader is wrong, their confusion tells you something about the writing.
- **High-leverage triage**: Not all feedback is equal. Sort comments into: (1) structural/argument-level (highest leverage; address first), (2) evidence/citation gaps (medium; often quick wins), (3) prose/style (lowest priority until the rest is fixed). Students often invert this order because prose comments are easier to act on.
- **What to do when you disagree**: You are allowed to disagree, but you must make a defensible choice. A useful rule: if a reader misread something, that is almost always a writing problem, not a reading problem. Either rewrite for clarity or add a signpost.
- **The "addressing reviewer comments" worksheet logic**: For each substantive comment, write: (a) what the reader said, (b) what you will do, (c) where in the manuscript you did it. This is professional practice and it forces you to act rather than ruminate.
- **Distinguish supervisor feedback from peer feedback**: Supervisor (Steven) feedback carries the assessment weight; peer feedback is for sense-checking and catching blind spots. Both are useful, but they have different functions.
- **Time-box the response**: Allocate revision time per comment proportional to its leverage, not its emotional weight.

## d) In-Class Peer Review Exercise Design (22 min)

**Format:** Pairs (trios only if odd numbers; trios get longer rotation). Students should bring **one section** of their Assignment #3 — ideally the empirical findings section, or whichever section they feel least confident about.

**Pre-class instruction (Agent 2 should put this on a slide and mention it as a reminder of what was already announced):** Bring a printed copy of one section (~5–8 pages) of your Assignment #3, or have it open on a laptop.

**Structure (22 min total):**
- 2 min — Steven introduces structure, hands out / displays prompt sheet
- 8 min — Silent reading: each student reads their partner's section while annotating against three prompts
- 10 min — Discussion in pairs (5 min per direction — set a timer): the *reader* talks first, the writer listens and takes notes; then switch
- 2 min — Quick whole-class debrief: 1–2 students share something useful they got

**Three structured reading prompts (these go on a worksheet slide / handout):**
1. **Argument trace.** In one sentence: what is the central claim of this section? Can you locate where the evidence supports it?
2. **Integration check.** Does this section visibly connect to the research question and the analytical framework laid out earlier in the thesis? Where does the connection feel weak or missing?
3. **One thing to keep, one thing to change.** Name a specific paragraph or move that works. Name one specific structural change (not a prose tweak) you would suggest.

**Critical pedagogical move:** The writer **listens without defending**. They take notes. This is the rule. Defending in the room burns the time and trains the wrong reflex for receiving Steven's written feedback.

**Worksheet:** A simple one-page template embedded in the slide deck (or referenced) with the three prompts and space for notes. Agent 3 may want to render this as a printable handout PDF or include it as an appendix slide students can photograph.

## e) Interactive Closing Exercise Design (8 min)

**Format chosen: Private "if-then" feedback plan (written reflection).**

**Why this format:** A think-pair-share leaks the emotional content (some students will not be honest about what they're worried about); a public triage exercise requires feedback they don't yet have. A private written exercise lets each student rehearse the response *before* the feedback arrives, when stakes are zero. It also produces an artifact they can pull out the day Steven's feedback drops.

**Structure:**
- 2 min — Steven explains the exercise and frames it ("this is for you, not for me")
- 5 min — Silent writing
- 1 min — Optional: anyone who wants to share, share

**Prompt (on slide):** "When you receive my feedback on Assignment #3, you will have about three weeks to act on it before the Final Manuscript is due. Write three if-then statements:

1. *If the feedback identifies a structural or argument-level problem,* then I will…
2. *If the feedback identifies missing evidence or a weak literature connection,* then I will…
3. *If I disagree with a comment,* then I will…

Add one sentence: what is the *first thing* you will do in the 24 hours after the feedback arrives?"

**Why these three:** They map onto the high-leverage triage taxonomy from Section 4. The 24-hour question is the decision-letter discipline made concrete.
